    6. Stretch your pitch

    1 min

    A wider pitch range makes you sound engaged. These feel silly. Do them anyway.

    • Count from one to ten, starting at your lowest comfortable note and going a little higher on each number. Then count back down.
    • Say "Really?" four ways: surprised, bored, angry, delighted.
    • Slide on "wow" from low to high, like a siren, then high to low.
    • Read one sentence from the passages, making the last word go down. Statements that end low sound sure; ones that rise sound like questions.

    Structures that make you sound clear

    Pick one before you speak. A structure stops rambling better than any amount of practice.

    PREP

    For opinions and answers

    1. Point. "I think we should launch in March."
    2. Reason. "Our biggest clients plan budgets in Q1."
    3. Example. "Last year three of them asked us in February."
    4. Point again. "So March gives us the best shot."

    STAR

    For stories and interview answers

    1. Situation. Where and when.
    2. Task. What you had to do.
    3. Action. What you did, using "I".
    4. Result. What changed, with a number if you have one.

    What, so what, now what

    For updates and meetings

    1. What. "Sign-ups dropped 10% this week."
    2. So what. "At this rate we miss the quarter."
    3. Now what. "I want to test a shorter form by Friday."

    Habits that work

    • Record yourself once a day and listen back. Uncomfortable, and the fastest way to improve.
    • Pause instead of filling. A one-second silence sounds confident. "Um" sounds unsure.
    • Lead with your point. Say the conclusion first, then explain it.
    • End sentences on a downward note. Rising at the end makes statements sound like questions.
    • Speak to the back of the room. Aim your voice past the person you're talking to.
    • Read aloud for 5 minutes a day. News articles, books, anything. It trains your mouth and your breath together.
    Speaking best practices

    What good speakers do differently

    Short, practical rules you can use in your next meeting. Pick one or two to focus on each week instead of trying everything at once.

    VoiceStructureWord choiceBody languageNervesMeetingsVideo callsBefore a big talk

    Voice

    • Speak at 130 to 160 words per minute.Most nervous speakers go above 180. Slow down further for the points that matter most.
    • Pause after important points.One or two seconds of silence lets an idea land. It sounds confident, not hesitant.
    • End statements on a falling tone.If your voice rises at the end, a statement sounds like a question and you sound unsure.
    • Stress one key word per sentence.Leaning on the word that carries the meaning keeps you from sounding flat.
    • Breathe from your belly.A low breath gives your voice power and steadiness. Shallow chest breathing makes it thin and fast.
    • Finish every word.Pronounce the final t, d and k sounds. Swallowed endings are the most common reason people say "sorry, what?"

    Structure

    • Lead with your point.Say the conclusion first, then explain it. Listeners can't scroll back up like readers can.
    • One idea per sentence.Long chains of "and then, and also" lose people. Finish the thought and stop.
    • Group things in threes.Three reasons, three steps, three examples. It's easy to remember and sounds complete.
    • Signpost where you're going."There are two reasons. First..." helps people follow and makes you sound organized.
    • Use one concrete example.A specific story or number is remembered long after general statements are forgotten.
    • End with a clear ask or summary.Don't trail off with "so, yeah." Finish with what you want people to do or remember.

    Word choice

    Say

    "I recommend we launch in March."

    "Here's what I found."

    "Good question. Let me think."

    (a short silent pause)

    "I don't know yet. I'll find out by Friday."

    Instead of

    "I just kind of think maybe we could launch in March?"

    "Sorry, this might be a dumb idea, but..."

    "Um, so, yeah, basically..."

    "Like, you know, actually..."

    "I'm not sure, I guess, it depends."

    • Use plain words."Use" beats "utilize". If a 12-year-old wouldn't understand it, simplify it.
    • Cut hedges and apologies."Just", "maybe", "sort of" and "sorry" quietly tell people not to take you seriously.
    • Prefer active voice."We missed the deadline" is clearer and more honest than "the deadline was missed".

    Body language

    • Stand or sit tall, feet flat.Good posture opens your chest and lets you breathe properly, which steadies your voice.
    • One thought, one person.Hold eye contact with one person for a sentence, about three to five seconds, then move to another.
    • Gesture between your shoulders and waist.Open, purposeful gestures in that zone look natural. Rest your hands when you're not using them.
    • Smile at the start and the end.It relaxes you and the room, and it changes the tone of your voice.
    • Stay still to make a point.Pacing and swaying drain attention. Move with purpose, then plant your feet for key lines.

    Handling nerves

    • Call it excitement.Nerves and excitement feel the same in your body. Telling yourself "I'm excited" works better than "calm down".
    • Breathe out longer than you breathe in.In for four, out for six, three times. It slows your heart rate within a minute.
    • Know your first 30 seconds cold.Nerves peak at the start. A well-practiced opening carries you until they settle.
    • If you go blank, pause.Take a sip of water, look at your notes, or repeat your last point. The audience barely notices a pause.
    • Focus on the listener, not yourself.Ask "what do they need to hear?" instead of "how do I look?" It takes the pressure off.

    Meetings and conversations

    • Listen to understand.Before you reply, sum up what they said in one line: "So the worry is timing?" People feel heard and you avoid talking past each other.
    • Keep your turns under a minute.Make your point, then invite a response. Long monologues lose the room.
    • Ask open questions."What would make this work for you?" gets far more than "Does this work?"
    • Disagree with "and", not "but"."I see the cost saving, and I'm worried about the timeline" keeps people listening.
    • Use people's names.It holds attention and makes the conversation feel personal.

    Video calls

    • Camera at eye level.Stack books under your laptop. Looking down into a camera reads as distant.
    • Look at the lens for key points.To the other person, that is eye contact.
    • Light in front of you.A window or lamp behind the screen, never behind you.
    • Use a headset or a decent mic.Clear audio matters more than video quality.
    • Bring a little more energy.Video flattens you. Speak slightly more expressively than you would in person.

    Before an important talk

    The day before
    • Say the whole thing out loud at least twice
    • Time it and cut anything that runs over
    • Write your first and last lines word for word
    • Guess three tough questions and prepare answers
    An hour before
    • Warm up: hum, tongue twisters, a few big yawns
    • Drink water, skip the extra coffee
    • Test your screen, slides and sound
    • Run through your opening once
    A minute before
    • Breathe in for four, out for six, three times
    • Roll your shoulders back, stand tall
    • Remind yourself of the one thing they must remember
    • Smile, pause, then begin
    Voice exercises

    Build a stronger, fuller voice

    A powerful voice comes from breath, resonance and a relaxed throat, not from pushing harder. Do these for 5 to 10 minutes a day; most people hear a difference within two to three weeks.

    Quick 5-minute warm-up
    1. Belly breathing
    2. Lip trills
    3. Humming
    4. Ha-ha pulses
    5. Counting to the wall

    Stop if anything hurts or your voice gets hoarse. These should feel easy. If hoarseness lasts more than two weeks, see a doctor or speech therapist.

    Test your breath control

    Take a full belly breath, then hold the sound as long as you can at an even volume. Try it before and after a few weeks of practice.

    Like a slow leak from a tyre. Keep it even, not loud.

    1 · Breath support

    Belly breathing

    2 min

    Breath is the fuel of your voice. Breathing low gives you power without strain.

    1. Put one hand on your chest and one on your belly.
    2. Breathe in through your nose for four. Only the belly hand should move.
    3. Breathe out slowly on "sss" for eight.
    4. Repeat. Keep your shoulders down the whole time.

    Ha-ha pulses

    1 min

    Teaches your belly muscles to push the sound out, so your throat doesn't have to.

    1. Hand on your belly, just below your ribs.
    2. Say "ha! ha! ha!" in short, sharp bursts. Feel your belly kick in on each one.
    3. Then try "Hey!", "Stop!", "Next!" with the same kick.
    4. Keep your throat loose. The power comes from below.
    2 · Resonance

    Humming

    1 min

    Resonance makes a voice sound full and warm instead of thin. Humming helps you find it.

    1. Lips lightly closed, teeth apart, hum "mmm" at a comfortable pitch.
    2. Feel the buzz on your lips and nose. If you feel it in your throat, relax and hum softer.
    3. Open into vowels: "mmm-ahh", "mmm-ohh", "mmm-eee".
    4. Keep the buzz going into each vowel.

    Find your natural pitch

    1 min

    Many people speak higher or lower than is natural for them, which tires the voice and sounds strained.

    1. Say "mm-hmm" as if you're casually agreeing with someone.
    2. The pitch of the "hmm" is close to your natural speaking pitch.
    3. Go straight into a sentence from it: "Mm-hmm, good morning, everyone."
    4. Repeat a few times until it feels automatic.

    Chest voice

    1 min

    A grounded, chest-resonant voice sounds calm and authoritative.

    1. Put your hand flat on your upper chest.
    2. Say "one, two, three" slowly in a relaxed, slightly lower voice.
    3. You should feel a gentle vibration under your hand.
    4. Read one sentence from a passage keeping that vibration. Don't force your pitch down; just relax.
    3 · Projection

    Counting to the wall

    2 min

    Being heard across a room comes from breath and focus, not shouting.

    1. Stand an arm's length from a wall and count one to ten in your normal voice.
    2. Step back to the middle of the room and count again, aiming your voice at the wall.
    3. Step back once more, to the far side of the room.
    4. Each time, take a bigger belly breath. Your throat should feel the same at every distance.

    The calling voice

    1 min

    The voice you'd use to call a friend across a park. It's loud but easy, because it's supported.

    1. Imagine a friend far away. Call "Hel-lo!" with a long, open vowel.
    2. Try "Over here!" and "Good morning!" the same way.
    3. Then say a normal sentence at half that volume, keeping the same open, forward feeling.
    4 · Warm-up and control

    Lip trills

    1 min

    The gentlest warm-up there is. Singers and actors use it before every performance.

    1. Relax your lips and blow through them so they flutter, like a motorboat "brrr".
    2. Add your voice and hold one steady pitch.
    3. Slide slowly from low to high and back, like a siren.
    4. If your lips won't flutter, press a fingertip into each cheek.

    Straw humming

    1 min

    Humming through a straw balances air pressure on your vocal cords, so they warm up with almost no effort.

    1. Put a regular drinking straw between your lips.
    2. Hum "ooo" through it at a comfortable pitch.
    3. Slide up and down slowly, like a siren.
    4. Then hum a simple tune you know.

    Pen-in-teeth reading

    2 min

    Makes your tongue and lips work harder, so you speak more clearly when the pen comes out.

    1. Hold a clean pen sideways, lightly between your front teeth.
    2. Read a passage aloud for one minute, trying to be as clear as possible.
    3. Take the pen out and read the same passage again.
    4. Notice how much crisper you sound.
    5 · Release tension

    Yawn and sigh

    1 min

    Tension in the throat is the biggest enemy of a strong voice. A yawn opens everything up.

    1. Take a big, slow yawn, letting your throat open wide.
    2. Halfway through, sigh it out on a gentle "haaah" from high to low.
    3. Repeat three to five times.

    Jaw and tongue release

    1 min

    A tight jaw muffles your voice and makes you mumble.

    1. Massage the hinge of your jaw, just in front of your ears, in small circles.
    2. Let your jaw hang loose and gently shake your head side to side.
    3. Stick your tongue out and down toward your chin for five seconds, then relax.
    4. Roll your shoulders back three times.

    Look after your voice

    • Drink water through the day.Your vocal cords need to be well hydrated. Sipping all day beats gulping before you speak.
    • Swallow instead of clearing your throat.Throat clearing slams your vocal cords together. A sip of water or a swallow works better.
    • Don't whisper to rest your voice.Whispering strains your voice more than soft speaking. Real rest means silence.
    • Warm up before long talks.Two minutes of lip trills and humming protects your voice like stretching before a run.
    • Avoid shouting over noise.Move closer, use a mic, or wait for the room to settle.
    • Sleep and steam.A tired body makes a tired voice. Breathing in steam from a hot shower soothes a dry throat.
